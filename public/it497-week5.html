01 / SPIRITUAL REFLECTION

Patience is part of the work.

When I think about becoming a better engineer, I usually think about technical skills. But some of the qualities I need most—patience, humility, and the willingness to serve—are the same ones I'm trying to develop spiritually.

I like solving problems, especially when I can see the result right away. When something takes longer than expected, though, I can become too focused on finishing it. I've learned that moving faster isn't always the same as doing something well.

This comes up in my instructional design work. When reviewing Canvas courses, I've found things that might seem small, like a homepage button pointing to the wrong week or an image missing alternative text. Fixing those details matters because a student might be relying on them. That is one way I can practice being more attentive to other people's needs.

“In your patience possess ye your souls.” (Luke 21:19)

Humility is another attribute I want to work on. I don't always have the right answer the first time, and I need to be willing to hear that. I see a connection with James 1:5: seeking wisdom starts with recognizing what I don't know.

Warning signs I'm learning to recognize

I'm rushing a task.

Instead of assuming a change is finished, I want to test the important parts and communicate anything I haven't verified.

I'm frustrated by slow progress.

I can step away for a moment, pray or reflect, and come back with a clearer plan rather than taking shortcuts.

I'm not listening well.

My next step should be to ask questions and consider feedback before defending my first idea.

I'm focused only on the result.

I want to ask who is affected by my work and whether the way I reached that result reflects my values.

What I want to carry into my final portfolio

My earlier portfolio already includes my guiding principles, résumé, projects, and an ethics case. For the final version, I want the examples to speak for themselves: what I built, why I made a particular decision, and what I still need to improve. I also want to make sure the way I present my work is as careful and honest as the work itself.

02 / DESIGN REVIEW

A portfolio should be easy to use.

My earlier version contained the right information, but it was crowded. There were too many navigation links, repeated headings, and a lot of separate cards. I wanted something that felt more like a personal portfolio and less like a collection of assignments.

I reviewed the previous page's HTML and responsive styles, checking the navigation targets, document structure, and accessibility features. This was a source-based review, not a formal user study. All eleven original navigation anchors pointed to existing sections, but the amount of navigation made the page harder to scan.

Clearer information hierarchy

I reduced the main navigation to six sections and used a single reading column for the reflections. The current work appears first; a link to my Week 4 portfolio preserves the longer archive.

A more restrained visual style

I replaced the large collection of cards, gradients, and colored boxes with consistent spacing, typography, and simple dividers. The focus is on the writing and projects rather than decoration.

Better keyboard and mobile support

I retained a skip-to-content link and visible keyboard focus, kept section anchors consistent, and adjusted the layout for narrow screens. The page also respects reduced-motion preferences.

More useful project context

Instead of repeating labels such as “technical approach” and “values in practice” in every card, I give each project a short description and a clear reason it matters.

How I checked it. The previous source review verified all eleven Week 4 navigation targets. For this version, I checked the HTML structure, navigation IDs, focus/skip-link features, and responsive CSS rules. These checks do not replace a manual test with a keyboard, mobile browser, or screen reader.

What I would test next. Ask someone unfamiliar with the site to find my résumé, locate the AI project, and read the ethics reflection on their phone. If they struggle, I would revise the navigation and spacing based on what they actually encounter.

03 / PROFESSIONAL ETHICS

Useful doesn't always mean appropriate.

One of the ethical cases I studied was the 2023 report that Samsung employees had shared internal work material with ChatGPT. I understand the temptation: when a tool can help solve a problem quickly, it's easy to focus on the answer and forget what information you're giving it.

But source code, meeting notes, and company information don't automatically belong in an outside service. The Samsung reports are a reminder that a convenient workflow can still create an unnecessary privacy or confidentiality risk (Wilkinson, 2023; Park, 2023).

If I faced something similar at work, I would check the company's policy first. If AI could help, I'd try an approved tool or a made-up example that doesn't include protected information. If I accidentally shared something confidential, I would report it promptly so the right people could respond. I wouldn't want to hide the mistake just because it was uncomfortable.

I also think companies should give employees realistic options. Training and approved tools make it easier to do the right thing; rules alone aren't always enough. This connects with the ACM Code of Ethics and its emphasis on privacy, avoiding harm, and professional responsibility (Association for Computing Machinery, 2018).

How it changed my own project

In assist_ai, my real-time AI assistant, I noticed that debugging information could include conversations. I changed the backend session logs to save basic operational data instead of full transcripts by default, removed unnecessary transcript printing, and added regression tests. The changes are committed, but the full test suite hasn't been independently verified yet. I don't want to present a planned or untested improvement as a finished guarantee.

04 / FAITH & WORK

What I believe should affect how I work.

I don't want faith to be a separate part of my life that has nothing to do with school or my future career. I'm trying to make the connection more practical—in how I treat people, solve problems, and take responsibility for the technology I build.

Elder David A. Bednar's message about gathering all things in Christ has helped me think of those parts of life as connected (Bednar, 2018). I'm studying AI and robotics because I enjoy solving technical problems, but I also want that work to help people. That's why things like accessibility, honest communication, and privacy matter to me.

I still have a lot to learn. For now, a practical goal is to take time for personal reflection and make sure my choices in work and school match what I say is important to me. This reflection doesn't claim a particular temple visit or personal spiritual experience; it records the connections I've been thinking about.
