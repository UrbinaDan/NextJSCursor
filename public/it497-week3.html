Task 1 / Spiritual Portfolio

Three guiding principles

These principles began in Week 1 and were expanded in Week 2. I want them to show up in the way I study, work, and treat other people—not just in a personal mission statement.

01

1. Small, consistent acts of faith

I want my spiritual life to be built on small choices that I can actually sustain. I sometimes want to see progress quickly, especially when I am learning a new technology or trying to solve a difficult programming problem. But the same patience I need to debug software is something I need in my spiritual life. Doctrine and Covenants 64:33–34 reminds me that important work starts with a foundation, and Alma 37:6–7 teaches that small and simple things can bring about great things. For me, that can mean making time for prayer and scripture study even when my schedule is full, and taking a few minutes to reflect on how I acted during the day. I do not expect every day to be perfect. What matters is returning to those habits and making gradual improvements. As an engineer, I can apply the same principle through testing, documentation, and continuous learning rather than relying on last-minute effort. My goal is to be consistent in both areas, not to treat faith and my career as competing responsibilities.

02

2. Integrity and stewardship

Integrity means that the work I present and the promises I make should match what I actually did. Stewardship means using my abilities, opportunities, and access to information responsibly. I connect this with the parable of the talents (Matthew 25:14–30), because I have been given educational and professional opportunities that I want to develop rather than waste. In software engineering, integrity has very concrete applications: being accurate about my role in a project, giving teammates credit, testing AI-generated responses before trusting them, and protecting confidential data. This matters even more in AI, where a tool can be powerful but can also produce mistakes or expose information when used without care. I want to be the kind of developer who admits limitations early, asks for permission when needed, and chooses a safer approach even if it takes more time. I can apply this principle in my portfolio by separating completed results from planned features and using evidence rather than exaggeration.

03

3. Christlike service and unity

I do not want to measure my future career only by a job title or how advanced a system is. I want to think about whether the technology actually helps someone. Mosiah 2:17 reminds me to connect service to God with the way I serve other people, and Doctrine and Covenants 121:41–42 teaches me that influence should come through patience, kindness, and sincere love. In team projects, this can look like listening to different viewpoints, explaining an idea without making someone feel small, and accepting feedback without becoming defensive. In my instructional design work, the people using the technology are students and instructors, so usability and reliability are not optional details; they affect a real learning experience. Elder Bednar’s (2018) image of strands woven into a rope helps me see how service, integrity, technical skill, and faith can strengthen one another. I want those values to guide my engineering decisions instead of appearing only in a separate statement on my website.

Selected technical projects

I want each project to show the problem, my role, technical choices, results, and how the work connects to responsible engineering.

PROJECT 01

PaperTheater AI — Scene Understanding Pipeline

Purpose & role. I developed an image-understanding pipeline that breaks a scene into structured layers to support physical paper-theater modeling.

Technical approach. The system combines segmentation, depth estimation, and generative AI ideas. The central challenge is translating one flat image into separated objects and layers that still make visual sense.

Outcome & evidence. The project demonstrates an integrated computer-vision workflow for generating structured scene layers. My next portfolio update will add a before-and-after example and a diagram showing how the stages connect.

Values in practice. This project reminds me to be honest about where model predictions may be imperfect. Integrity means checking results and explaining limitations instead of presenting every generated output as correct.

PROJECT 02

assist_ai — Real-Time AI Assistant

Purpose & role. I built an AI assistant with conversational, note-taking, and task-oriented workflows designed to use context during an interaction.

Technical approach. I used Python, FastAPI, WebSockets, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), and LLM APIs. The challenge was connecting real-time communication with contextual information in a backend that could support multiple tasks.

Outcome & evidence. The project demonstrates API design, real-time backend development, and AI integration. For stronger evidence, I plan to publish a simple architecture diagram and a safe demonstration without personal data.

Values in practice. Working with contextual information makes privacy and stewardship especially important. I want to minimize unnecessary data exposure and be clear about what the assistant knows and what it only predicts.

PROJECT 03

Canvas QA Automation — Course Content Validation

Purpose & role. I created an API-driven tool that checks Canvas course content against quality rules and generates feedback that can be used to correct issues.

Technical approach. The system applies repeatable validation to course pages and related content. Manual review can be inconsistent across large courses, so the design focuses on identifying problems in a clear, systematic way.

Outcome & evidence. The result is a validation workflow that produces actionable feedback. I plan to share a sanitized sample report rather than private course or student data.

Values in practice. This connects to Christlike service because course tools affect real students and instructors. Reliability, accessibility, and protecting educational information are ways my technical work can support other people.

Ethical case: Samsung employees and ChatGPT (2023)

In April 2023, reports described employees in Samsung Electronics’ semiconductor business entering internal information into ChatGPT while trying to complete work. One reported example involved source code submitted to help solve a programming problem, another involved code submitted for optimization, and a third involved material from an internal meeting used to produce notes (Wilkinson, 2023). The employees may have been trying to save time or solve technical problems, but the information belonged to the company. The people involved included the employees, Samsung as the custodian of the confidential material, and the external AI provider that received the prompts. This is an example of an unintended disclosure to a third-party service; the reporting does not establish that the source code was subsequently published to the general public.

Samsung’s initial response was to temporarily restrict generative AI services on company-issued devices and on devices connected to internal networks while it evaluated safer ways to use AI (Park, 2023). I understand why the company acted quickly, but this incident also shows that banning a tool alone is not a complete long-term answer. Developers still need practical rules, approved tools, and training that explain what kinds of data can be entered into external services and what to do when an incident happens.

From a professional ethics perspective, the ACM Code of Ethics asks computing professionals to honor confidentiality, respect existing rules, and evaluate the risks of computer systems (Association for Computing Machinery [ACM], 2018). Sharing proprietary code with an unapproved service can violate those duties even when the intention is to improve productivity. In my own work, I would first check company policy, avoid uploading private code or personal information, and use an approved internal tool or a synthetic example whenever possible. If I had already shared something confidential by mistake, I would report it promptly instead of hoping no one noticed. This dilemma connects directly to my principle of integrity and stewardship: being responsible with information is part of respecting the people and organizations who trust me. As I pursue AI engineering, I want to balance innovation with careful judgment rather than assuming that a useful tool is automatically appropriate for every task.

Task 3 / Personal Reflection

Temple-focused reflection

Thinking about the temple and my covenants helps me remember that my life is bigger than school deadlines or finding the right job. At this stage, I am working toward graduation and thinking seriously about my career in AI and robotics. Those are important goals, but it is easy for them to take over my attention. As I reflect on temple worship, I think about promises, the direction of my life, and the kind of person I want to become. The thought that stands out to me is that progress should not be measured only by the number of projects I finish or the skills I add to my résumé. I also want to be patient, keep my commitments, and make time for my family and for the Lord. That idea connects with 3 Nephi 13:19–22, because the things I value most will influence my daily choices.

I also see a practical connection between these spiritual goals and my professional growth. I can be ambitious about engineering without treating achievement as the only measure of success. When a project becomes difficult, I can respond with humility instead of frustration. When I work with another person, I can listen and help rather than focus on getting credit. When I use AI tools, I can think about trust and privacy, even if a shortcut would be faster. Elder Bednar’s (2018) rope analogy has become useful to me because I do not want my spiritual life, education, and future career to be separate pieces that only occasionally touch. I want them to support one another. My takeaway is to keep building small habits that strengthen my faith and also make me a more dependable engineer: honest reporting, regular reflection, and choices that put people before convenience.
